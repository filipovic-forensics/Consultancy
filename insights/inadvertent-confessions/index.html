Inadvertent Confessions: What Criminal Defense Attorneys Should Know

By Dr Luna Filipovic · Based on peer-reviewed research published in Frontiers in Psychology (2021)

A suspect can appear to admit guilt without intending to confess. Equally, an investigator may believe an admission has occurred when the interview record does not support that interpretation. Understanding how this happens requires careful attention to language in context.

What is an inadvertent confession?

An inadvertent confession or admission arises when the speaker is not fully aware that their words are being taken as a confession or admission, or when the listener infers guilt that the speaker has not in fact acknowledged. This differs from treating every disputed confession as a coerced or deliberately false confession. Inadvertent admissions can arise through miscommunication, and coercion is not a necessary condition.

Why does this matter in police interviews?

Interviewers and suspects routinely draw inferences from what is said, rather than relying only on explicit statements. Problems arise when those inferences do not match the speaker’s meaning and the misunderstanding is not resolved. The precise sequence of questions and answers, the context of the exchange, and opportunities to check understanding may all matter when interpreting an apparent admission.

What does the research show?

My published study examined 50 police interviews from the United Kingdom and 50 interrogations from the United States, including both English-speaking and non-English-speaking suspects. It used discourse analysis to investigate how inadvertent confessions and admissions occur. The findings identify inferential misunderstandings as well as linguistic, cultural and procedural factors. The study also discusses the importance of checking understanding and the quality of interpreting in police questioning.

Not only a translation problem

Inadvertent admissions can occur even when everyone speaks the same language. Interpreter-mediated questioning may introduce additional risks, but misaligned interpretations can also arise through ordinary conversational inference, question formulation and the dynamics of an interview. The distinction matters: reviewing only a translated sentence, or only an isolated excerpt, may overlook how its meaning developed in the interaction.

Questions attorneys may wish to consider

  • Does the recorded exchange support the alleged confession or admission when read in its full conversational context?
  • Was the suspect’s understanding checked, particularly where questions or answers were ambiguous?
  • Were the original-language statements and any interpretations or translations preserved for review?
  • Could differences between the speaker’s intended meaning and the investigator’s inference be relevant to the case?

These are analytical questions, not conclusions about any particular case. An independent linguistic assessment depends on the available recordings, transcripts and other evidence.
