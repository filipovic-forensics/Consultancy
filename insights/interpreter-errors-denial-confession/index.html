How Interpreter Errors Can Turn a Denial into an Apparent Confession

By Dr Luna Filipovic · Based on peer-reviewed research published in Interpreting (2022)

Can someone deny responsibility for an alleged crime in one language, only to appear to admit responsibility in another? My research on police interpreting in the United Kingdom and the United States shows how differences between original statements and their interpretation can profoundly affect the linguistic evidence available to investigators.

When “I dropped her” does not mean what investigators think

Consider a real-life homicide investigation examined in my research, in which a Spanish-speaking suspect described carrying an unconscious woman down stairs, where she sustained fatal head injuries. Whether the woman was dropped intentionally or fell accidentally was a crucial distinction.

The suspect used the Spanish construction se me cayó, which presents the fall as non-intentional. A family member acting as interpreter rendered the suspect’s response in English as “Yes, I dropped her.”

The English verb drop can describe an intentional or accidental event. But the investigator was explicitly asking whether the suspect had dropped the woman or whether she had fallen. In that context, the English rendition appeared to confirm an intentional act, while the Spanish construction conveyed a non-intentional event.

The original words and the interpretation available to investigators did not convey the same implication about intention. This distinction could be highly consequential in a homicide investigation.

Why professional, impartial interpreting is essential

Legal interpreting must be entrusted to qualified, impartial and neutral language professionals with specialist training in legal interpreting.

Being bilingual does not make someone a qualified legal interpreter. Accurate interpreting in police interviews and interrogations requires more than fluency in two languages. It requires specialist linguistic knowledge, familiarity with legal terminology and procedures, professional interpreting skills, and a commitment to neutrality and impartiality.

Police officers, family members and other untrained bilingual individuals should not be used as substitutes for independent professional interpreters. They may lack the skills required to preserve crucial distinctions in meaning. Personal or institutional interests may also compromise, or create concerns about, their impartiality.

My comparative study examined 100 authentic interpreter-assisted police interviews and interrogations: 50 from the UK and 50 from the US. The UK cases involved professional interpreters; the US cases frequently involved bilingual police officers or family members. In 27 of the 50 US cases, the analysis identified changes of meaning and omissions, sometimes repeatedly within the same case. The UK data showed substantially fewer problems attributable to interpreting itself.

These findings do not mean that every interpreting difference results in a false confession. They show that interpreting problems can change significant evidential details and may remain unresolved. Professional, independent and impartial legal interpreting is fundamental to obtaining reliable linguistic evidence and protecting the fairness of legal proceedings.

When a transcript conceals important evidence

The study also revealed important differences in how interviews were documented. The US transcripts examined were bilingual and verbatim, recording original-language statements, interpreting and subsequent independent control translations. This allowed discrepancies to be identified.

The UK transcripts examined were generally English-only and non-verbatim, sometimes omitting important parts of the original communication. In one UK case involving a Portuguese-speaking suspect, an ambiguity surrounding the English verb drop contributed to a misunderstanding. The suspect’s response implicitly challenged the allegation, but this was not recognized by the interviewing officer and was not fully recorded in the transcript.

The lesson is that professional interpreting and reliable documentation are complementary safeguards. A detailed transcript can help reveal problems after an interview, but it cannot undo misunderstandings that affected the interview as it unfolded.

What should attorneys look for?

  • Was the interpreter a qualified, independent language professional, or a police officer, family member or other untrained bilingual speaker?
  • Does the record preserve the speaker’s original words as well as the interpretation provided to investigators?
  • Could differences between the languages have changed the meaning of a statement, especially concerning intention, responsibility or denial?
  • Was an independent control translation prepared?
  • Were ambiguities identified and clarified during the interview?

These are questions for assessing the available evidence, not conclusions about any particular case. An apparent admission in English should be considered in light of the original-language interaction whenever that evidence is available.

The central lesson for legal professionals

The reliability of multilingual evidence begins with the quality of the interpreting. Qualified legal interpreters must be able to preserve meaning accurately, recognize ambiguities, request clarification when necessary and remain neutral throughout the communication process.

Professional interpreting should be accompanied by appropriate quality-control measures and accurate, bilingual, verbatim records in important cases. My research recommends this combination as a way to improve both the quality of evidence-gathering and the ability to scrutinize linguistic evidence later.

The goal is not simply to make communication possible. It is to ensure that the evidence obtained is accurate, reliable and capable of withstanding scrutiny in court.
